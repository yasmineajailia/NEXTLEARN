02 — Master of the business domain

Higher-education e-learning: actors, content, and the catch-up stake.

Actors

Three roles, two interfaces

Students follow the course and take quizzes; teachers manage classes, content access and monitoring; administrators manage teachers. Student front-office, teacher back-office.

Content model

Module → acquis → sous-acquis

The C course is structured in competency blocks. Each sous-acquis carries course material (PDF), videos and a quiz with two attempts and a 60% pass mark; passing unlocks the next prerequisites on a weekly calendar.

The stake

Rattrapage (catch-up exams)

Students who fail the semester sit a catch-up exam — costly for them and the school. The whole domain problem is spotting the trajectory toward rattrapage while there is still time to change it.

What the domain data looks like

Behavioral traces: lesson completions, quiz scores and timestamps, login frequency, self-evaluations — plus, with consent, focus scores from attention tracking. No demographic data is used.

Domain constraints that shaped the design

Pedagogy must stay explainable (a teacher cannot act on a black box), webcams are ethically sensitive (processing must stay local), and the course is French-first with an English UI option.

03 — Investigation on the real plan

What exists today, and where it stops.

LMS

Moodle

The reference open-source LMS. Complete but heavy, and its analytics stay descriptive; risk plugins exist but are unexplained black boxes.

Virtual class

Google Classroom

Assignment distribution and communication. No prediction, no recommendation, no adaptation — no pedagogical intelligence at all.

MOOC

Coursera / OpenClassrooms

Massive generic catalogs. Cannot host a school's own program, supports and evaluations; recommendations serve the catalog, not the learner.

Adaptive

Khan Academy

Mastery-based adaptive exercises — closest to our vision, but a closed catalog with no institutional content and no explainable analytics.

Four shared blind spots

No explained early-warning signal · recommendations disconnected from how the student learns · no measure of real engagement (a "viewed" video proves nothing) · assistants not grounded in the school's actual course.

04 — Problem posed

Failure is detected at the exam — when nothing can be done anymore.

Too late

The teacher discovers who is in difficulty from the final grades. Every week of delay makes remediation harder and rattrapage more likely.

Unexplained

Even when a risk score exists, neither the student nor the teacher knows which behavior drives it — so no one knows what to change.

Impersonal

Same path, same supports, same help for every student, regardless of their learning style, their gaps or their actual engagement.

Question: can we predict, early and per module, which students are heading to catch-up exams and what grade they are heading toward — explain each prediction in actionable terms, and adapt the platform's guidance accordingly?

08 — TDSP methodology

The Team Data Science Process, applied end to end.

1

Business understanding

Domain study, BOs → DSOs mapping, success metrics fixed before modeling.

2

Data acquisition & understanding

Behavioral traces from the platform + calibrated synthetic training data; 7-feature vector per student per module.

3

Modeling

Two Random Forests (classification + regression), K-means profiling; seeded, scripted, reproducible training.

4

Deployment

Models served in-process (<100 ms); SHAP microservice with graceful degradation; RAG index auto-rebuilt on content change.

5

Customer acceptance

Teacher dashboards, per-student explanations, live demo — validated against the BOs.

Iterative in practice

The loop ran several times: the grade model, module-scoped predictions and the exact-tree SHAP reconstruction were later iterations triggered by acceptance feedback on earlier versions.

Reproducibility as discipline

Fixed seeds, versioned training and evaluation scripts (train, evaluate, fresh-data test), and honest metric reporting are enforced by the project's own conventions.
